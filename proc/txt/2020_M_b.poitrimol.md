# **Introduction**

Driven by my interest regarding the field of robotics, I came across many types of

robots and their even more numerous applications. Although I had some knowledge

about things like AI or kinematics, I felt that I was still lacking. Thus, this work

represented a great opportunity for me to deepen my knowledge and sharpen my skills

in robotics.

When I was still looking for a suitable subject for my research, professor Igarashi taught

me about a particular type of parallel robots that uses cables instead of typical rigid

links. I was very intrigued by this type of robots, and I decided to familiarize with their

unique features and all their potential applications.

I then searched and found out that they could be used as haptic interfaces to interact

with virtual reality environment. At this point, I wondered what I could do to improve

the performance of already existing interfaces such as SPIDAR. I found out that, even

if cable-based interfaces’ strong point is their adaptable workspace, the high number of

cable used can become a hindrance for the user.

Increasing the overall dimensions of the workspace, as well as reducing the number of

cables, would not only prevent the cables from colluding but would also guarantee the

immersion of the user. Finding a solution that provides such benefits was far enough to

make a research subject that would last for a year and a half.

The solution I propose in this paper was inspired by hybrid robots using both rigid links

and cables. Such robots had already been designed and used as typical manipulators but

I still had to adapt the design and control algorithm to fit the requirements of haptic

interfaces.

# 第 1 章 Haptic interfaces

The field of Virtual Reality (VR) and its applications have been thoroughly researched

during the past decades. And improving the user ’ s immersion in the virtual world, while

allowing complex interaction, is still a major challenge nowadays. SPIDAR-like cable

haptic interfaces can display forces accurately and ensure a satisfying haptic rendering

quality, though many cables can be required to provide feedback on both hands.

In this chapter, we will give some context regarding VR and haptic interfaces and review

the many interfaces designed throughout the years. Then we will talk about the SPIDAR

interface and previous works inspired by it. This will then lead to the definition of the

problematic and the solution chosen to address it.

2

### **1.1 Context**

A general definition of Virtual Reality (VR) could be the following: VR displays via

a computer artificial objects and motions, while trying to make them look as realistic

as possible [1]. The goal of any VR application is to progressively immerse the user

so that he can become an actor of this interactive world. The most common examples

of Virtual Reality, like video games, use simple input devices such as joysticks or pads

which implies that the feedback is very limited (vibrations at most)[2].

Fig. 1.1: Forecast of haptic interfaces market size [3]

Despite the apparent simplicity of the interactions permitted so far, VR has been

trending more and more in recent years. As shown by the graph below (see 1.1), the

global VR market is expected to grow steadily over the years and it has been doing so

for at least five years straight. This shows an increase in interest towards technologies

related to VR interaction.

Moreover, this trend is not only restricted to a particular field. Obviously, when the

acronym VR is pronounced what comes to mind is entertainment and video games.

Figure 1.2 presented on the next page confirms this trend. However, VR and its appli
cations are not limited to this field but can also be seen in the automotive industry, in

the aerospace industry or for healthcare purposes[4].

3

Fig. 1.2: Forecast of Japan’s VR headset market size by application [4]

Note that in this case, we make the link between VR headsets and haptic interfaces

market. The reason is simple: both share the same applications and complete each other

in the fact that they are often used together to immerse the user in a virtual world.

Alongside the development of numerous new technologies to improve the representation

of the virtual environment [5], this lack of interaction has been tackled by many researches

in the form of haptic interfaces.

### **1.2 Different types of interfaces**

It would not be an understatement to say that haptic interfaces are almost as numerous

as their applications. Even some new ways to provide haptic feedback have been proposed

in the last decade such as lasers[6].

For the sake of simplicity, we will only describe the most widely used and first interfaces

developed: rigid and cable-based interfaces. Indeed, as soon as the early nineties, both

rigid and cable-based haptic interfaces have been proposed.

**1.2.1** **Rigid interfaces**

Since they’re not the main focus of our research we will only briefly talk about rigid

interfaces (a more complete list will be given at the end of this section). Rigid interfaces

are the most common type of haptic interfaces: they are basically designed as robots

arms, with a part the user can utilize to interact with the environment through the robot

called the end-effector. Typically a stylus or a shape which is easy to grasp is used as

an effector.

Fig. 1.3: 3D systems’ haptic interface: Touch[8]

5

The commercially available (and very often used in research) PHANToM [7] illustrates

what a rigid haptic interface is very well (se$e_{1}$.3).

It has a simple design of robotic arm with a stylus at the end that can be used to

interact with virtual objects. It has the advantages of its simple architecture being a

linear chain of links and motors which implies that the kinematic model is easier to

determine (as opposed to parallel robots for instance). It can also provide decent force

feedback while being intuitive to use thanks to its shape.

Regarding the downsides, we can see that the workspace is very limited and not suitable

for applications requiring a powerful feedback. Also, when a VR headset is not used the

immersion of the user is greatly reduced by the presence of the robot in his field of vision.

Lastly, the robot is not easily reconfigurable: it cannot be adapted to the application

freely.

6

**1.2.2** **Cable-based interfaces**

Regarding the problems pointed out regarding rigid interfaces, cable-driven parallel

mechanism (or cable-based interfaces) can provide relatives advantages due to their par
ticular features. An example of interface[9] controlled with an admittance based scheme

is shown in 1.4.

The first advantage is the scalability of the device: the simple structure (cbles, motors

and frame) of cable-based interfaces allows for more reconfigurability and large-scale

haptic rendering. Also, considering their low intrusiveness and low inertia, cable-based

devices have been used for many applications such as radiotelescopes [10] and, of course,

haptic interfaces.

Despite these advantages, cable haptic devices all share the same disadvantages and

constraints [11].

The system has to be over constrained, usually the number of cables is equal to the

number of wrench existing degree of freedom +1. Using more cables and motors can

help improving the control on the end-effector, but doing so would induce potential

cable interferences and impact the ability of the user to reach the effector.

The cable tension has to remain positive during the task. Slacking the cables would

require a specific computation method which could hinder the real time position

tracking of the end effector.

The workspace of the device is smaller than its span; usually cable robots are large

scale devices in order to increase the workspace and the range of forces that can be

displayed. As a consequence, these cannot be used as desktop devices due to their

size and loss in actual workspace. Rigid interfaces would be preferred despite their

structural weight, lack of mechanical transparency and intrusiveness in the virtual

environment.

Anyway we can describe every single interface and its variation since there are so many

(se$e_{1}$.5). Nonetheless, one interface and its evolutions over the years have been a pioneer

in the field and inspired many work: we will present it in the next section.

### **1.3 SPIDAR**

The cable-based haptic interface called SPIDAR ( SPace Interface Device for Artificial

Reality) was introduced by Sato et al. back in 1992 [15]. It consists in a really simple

structure made of a frame, four motor, four cables and a finger cap that allows the user

to use a finger tip to interact with virtual objects. The length of each wire is measured

by a rotary encoder built on each motor and the haptic force is generated by controlling

the tension of each string. Figure 1.6 shows how simple yet practical the design of the

SPIDAR is.

Fig. 1.6: Representation of SPIDAR[16]

To simulate the sense of touch, or haptic feedback, the SPIDAR uses the virtual

perception cycle described on the next page(se$e_{1}$.7). To put it simply, the user moves

the fingert tip until it touches the virtual object. Then, the movement tracked by the

encoder will trigger a force feedback in real time. Finally, the association between the

force felt through the finger tip and the visual feedback (provided by a display or VR

headset for example) will create the illusion of touching a real solid object.

9

Fig. 1.7: Virtual perception cycle [15]

10

In the last section, we described the features shared by cable-based interfaces. The

SPIDAR also share the same advantages and disadvantages: particularly its limited

workspace. Although it is indeed really easy to move the motors around to change the

configuration, with for motors only half of the workspace inside the frame can be used.

It can be misleading for a user since you can’t clearly know where the boundaries of the

workspace are. Furthermore, it constrains the size of the object that can be interacted

with. In this regard, the use of the workspace can be considered as not optimal.

Fig. 1.8: Workspace and force display[16]

The figure 1.8 above illustrates this fact. It can be observed that the range of haptic

display is limited to a tetrahedron. Concretely, the resultant force vector represented by *f* *[⃗]*

that provides the haptics cannot by exerted in every direction outside of the tetrahedron.

The optimization of the workspace will be the first and main objective of our work.

11

Over the years, the SPIDAR has inspired many systems and has itself seen many

evolutions[14]. Nonetheless, what we have said before can still be applied to newer sys
tems.

In fact, even if the most advanced versions of SPIDAR now propose multi-finger inter
action a new problem has appeared. If we look at the figure 1.9 below we can see that

the number of cables used is growing more and more, while the workspace is not getting

bigger but tends to get smaller (see SPIDAR-8 in figur$e_{1}$.11).

Fig. 1.9: Table illustrating the evolution of the SPIDAR[14]

The increasing number of cables introduces the problem of cable interferences (or

collision between cables) when it did not exist before. Thus introducing a new design to

reduce this number of cables seems to be necessary.

The SPIDAR-10 (figur$e_{1}$.10) could be considered as a first innovation in terms of design

because it allows the rotation of the frame which was not possible with previous models.

Nonetheless, the problem of the number of cables is even more flagrant in this case.

The second objective of our work will be to try to find an innovative design to reduce

the number of cables.

### **1.4 Previous works**

Needless to say that the SPIDAR was the main inspiration behind the work presented

in this paper. But there were at least two other works that provided interesting and rele
vant informations. The first one[13] is a novel interface which gave some hints regarding

the control scheme. The second work more or less inspired the design of our system[18].

The work done by Saint-Aubert et al.[13] outlines the design and the implementation

of a device combining a stereoscopic 3D display below which the manipulation space is

located (see 1.13). Basically the system superimposes, visual and haptic feedbacks to

improve the immersion.

In this paper the process to simplify the distribution of forces is detailed (see 1.13). It

consists in using smaller Jacobian matrix depending on the position of the end effector.

This is done by dividing the workspace by areas in order to get square matrices easy to

invert which will as a direct consequence, reduce the computation time of the control

algorithm.

We also understood from this paper that in most other works, the control algorithm

is either based on impedance control or not even detailed (or too complicated for real

time application). That is why we need to find a simple control algorithm that allows

real time feedback for our system.

The second paper [18]presents Sophia-3 (string-operated planar haptic interface for arm

rehabilitation), described by the authors as a planar cable-driven device with a tilting

working plane. This cable robot, by adding a moving anchor point, represents a solu
tion to reduce the number of cable of a parallel cable-driven robot(described b$y_{1}$.13).

However, neither the control method nor the application are relevant for our work: the

objective is to help the reeducation of disabled patients. In our case, we want free

movement like the SPIDAR and haptic interaction with objects only.

We can only use the concept but we need to show that it can be used for haptic with

a simple control method

### **1.5 Purpose of this work**

The purpose of this research is to enlarge the workspace of a small scale cable haptic

device, while keeping the number of cables as low as possible and ensuring a realistic

force display.

A planar n+1 cable haptic device is proposed, its workspace is enlarged to match the

workspace of a square n+2 planar cable robot. A linear actuator is added to the system,

translating the workspace during the manipulation to follow the position of the end

effector.

17

**1.5.1** **Restrictions**

In order to reduce the cost and complexity of the method required, we chose to design

a planar interface. Our system is supposed to show the feasibility of using hybrid design

using both cables and parallel links as a SPIDAR-like haptic interface.

Thus, once the proof of concept exists it can be adapted to 3D interfaces and multi-finger

interfaces in future works.

18

**1.5.2** **Objectives**

The objectives of our work have been described and determined through the studies

and works presented in the previous sections. They are the following:

The system has to be simple and close to the SPIDAR in terms of design and haptic

feedback provided.

The control scheme used need to be detailed and simple enough to allow real time

force feedback.

The workspace of the system has to occupy as much of the space inside the frame

as possible.

The force feedback should be accurate and done without using sensors if possible.

19

### **2.1 Conception**

**2.1.1** **Design**

The system has to be as compact as possible to fit on a desk while limiting parasitic

forces (inertia, friction) thatcould cause vibrations and degrade the quality of the display.

The force feedback (ie. the motors) has to convey sufficient continuous forces of about

5N [13] without impacting the free motion of the effector when feedback is not needed.

As said before, 2D design was chosen to avoid more complex design considerations and

to act as a proof of concept that such hybrid devices can be used as haptic displays.

Considering the above mentioned constraints, choosing direct drive high torque motors

helps avoiding backlash and high forces can be displayed on the handle to reproduce the

sensation of touching a hard object like a wall for example. It also facilitates the tuning

of the control method we will present later in section 4. The inertia and friction will

impact the user sensation but a compromise was necessary between system transparency

and range of force display. Finally, the device is easily reconfigurable so that several

configurations can be tested.

The haptic interface designed for this research is shown i$n_{2}$.1 (note that the effector

is superimposed with the object in this image: it’s only the case because the effector is

stopped at the origin but is NOT represented by the green sphere in the VR simulation)

. It is a 50 *cm* in length, 30 *cm* in width and 40 *cm* in height planar cable robot with a

typical n+1 triangular configuration. The manipulation workspace is about 30 *∗* 30 *cm* [2]

and is perpendicular to the ground to gain more space so that the whole system fits on

a desktop. The proposed system consists of three direct drive brushless DC motors used

to control the cables, and one AC servo motor which controls the linear actuator.

### **2.2 Kinematics**

**2.2.1** **Conventional model**

The kinematic model we used is commonly used in other researches and is largely

inspired by the model introduced by Williams et al.[11]. The configuration of the device

and the associated geometric model are shown i$n_{2}$.6. The workspace of the device is

equilateral and its sides are of fixed length *A* . Each of the three cables passes through

anchor points denoted *{M* *i* *x M* *i* *y}* *[T]* ; with a variable length *L* *i* and angles *$\theta$* *i* ( *i* = 1, 2,

3). The cable pulley radius, which is not shown, is denoted *r* and is the same for all

cables. The components of position vectors are expressed in ( *M* 1, *X* 0, *Y* 0 ) and the end

effector is considered as a point with its associated ( *x*, *y* ) coordinates. The coordinate

system attached to the end-effector is purely indicative as it is only used to simplify the

formulas when matching the device to the simulation in Unity. *X* *L* corresponds to the

position of the linear actuator. When initializing the system, the end-effector is placed

at the centroid of the triangle so that it stays in place when putting the cables in tension.

It can be done quite precisely without requiring advanced calibration method contrary

to 3D systems.

The position of the handle is obtained from each wire’s length *Li* since these lengths

are known from the motors encoder. By writing equation (2.1) for each case, a system

of three equations with two unknowns is obtained.

*L* *i* = ~~√~~ ( *x −* *M* *i* *x* ) [2] + ( *y −* *M* *i* *y* ) [2] (2.1)

Then the (x, y) coordinates of the end effector can be written of two out of three

cables (2.2). In our case, the data from the two first motors (respectively *M* 1 and *M* 2 )

are used due to the fact that the x-coordinate of the linear actuator is matched with *M* 3

and variates during operation.

# 第 3 章 Force feedback

In this chapter, we will describe the mechanisms used to feedback the forces through

the cables in order to give a feeling of touch to the user. More precisely, the Disturbance

Observer (DOB) and Reaction Torque Observe (RTOB) used to evaluate the force will

be described. Then we will explain how to distribute the force between the cables.

31

### **3.1 Motor control**

This section describes the method used to estimating the reaction force in the effector.

Instead of using a force sensor such as a torque sensor to estimate the reaction force, a

method called Reaction Torque Observer (RTOB) is used to make the system sensorless.

This not only reduces the cost of the sensor, but also reduces the physical interference

of the sensor on the end-effector, thus improving the immersion. However, when using

RTOB, if there is noise in the output value, it cannot be determined whether the output

value is an error or a reaction force. Therefore, it is necessary to filter the error. So

we will first explain how functions the Disturbance OBserver (DOB) that can eliminate

disturbances.

32

**3.1.1** **Disturbance Observer**

The disturbance estimation observer (DOB) proposed by Onishi et al.[19] is a method

that enables the removal of disturbances by comparing the command value and the

model value. Figure 3.1 shows the block diagram of DOB and RTOB used to control the

motors, note that we use a PD controller. In the model, the acceleration is calculated

by multiplying the mass term *J* by the value obtained by multiplying the position by

the value of the PD controller. We chose to neglect the viscosity coefficient *B* . However,

it does not work according to the model because the disturbance term $\delta$ *∗* $\delta$ *s* +1 [exists in]

the operation. Therefore, it is possible to calculate the difference of one feedback loop

as a disturbance by using the nominal value *J* *n* obtained from the system identification.

Then it is possible to remove the disturbance by subtracting that value.

33

Fig. 3.1: DOB and RTOB block diagram

34

**3.1.2** **Reaction Torque Observer**

As in the previous section, the disturbance was removed by comparing with the value

obtained from the system identification using the disturbance observer. However, when a

force is generated, it cannot be completely removed by the disturbance observer. There
fore, the reaction torque applied to the actuator is estimated by introducing a reaction

torque observer. As shown in the block diagram shown in Figure 3.1, the reaction torque

observer (RTOB) estimates the reaction torque by comparing it with the model value

again after DOB[20]. This makes it possible to estimate the reaction torque applied to

the actuator without using a force sensor and for each motor.

35

### **4.2 Data workflow**

The data workflow of the system is described in 4.2 on the next page

### **4.3 Block diagram**

The block diagram of the control algorithm is shown in Fig.4.3. It consists in a closed
loop position controller with three main parts: the Disturbance Observer (DOB) and

Reaction Torque Observer (RTOB) part, the actuator’s control loop, and the feedback

part with the virtual model. The Disturbance Observer (DOB) helps reducing inherent

perturbations and parasitic noise, while the RTOB outputs the force exerted by the user

on the end-effector denoted ˆ *$\tau$* *i* .

We chose to use a purely inertial nominal model for the motors and frictions are not

compensated in the loop. The output coming from the RTOB is an input for the virtual

model that defines the behavior of the object. The virtual model is a mass-damping
stiffness ( *M*, *D*, and *K* ) model as described in (4.1).

The actuator is controlled in a separate loop, basically the actuator follows the end

# 第 5 章 Experiments and results

Two main experiments were conducted to validate our solution: the first one aims at

showing that the workspace was indeed enlarged, and the second have to check that the

forces used for haptic feedback are accurate and realistic. In this chapter we will also

discuss briefly the methods to evaluate the features of a haptic interface.

### **5.1 Reduction of user-felt manipulation resistance**

In this part, we evaluate the workspace and the resistance manipulation which is

inferred from the reaction forces we computed.

Figur$e_{5}$.1 shows the manipulation resistance during manipulation. The values have

been measured and range between 0N and 4.6N. In fact, for tensions not exceeding 5N

during manipulation for free movements the input force of the user isn ’ t surpassed[13].

The measurements have been done by moving the end-effector to each available point of

the workspace and measuring the current output (mapped using the jacobian).

As expected, a typical tension distribution already presented in other works[18] can

be observed. The limits of the workspace where the tension is maximal corresponds to

the singularities of the system. On the contrary, when the actuator is allowed to move,

a different distribution can be observed (see 5.2). A red portion with high tension can

be observed on the top right corner of the graph. This is due to the placement of the

motors on the frame of the device. A displacement in the position of the workspace limit

can be observed.

Indeed, although the bottom limit doesn ’ t move, the overall workspace is widened by

approximately 60%. This shows that even if the configuration of the device is important,

the gain in term of haptic rendering workspace is not negligible.

# 第 6 章 Conclusion

In this paper we presented a hybrid cable robot that can be used to interact with

virtual objects and eventually graps them when the concept wil be adapted to 3D. After

a short summary, we will explain the future prospects and improvements that could be

added to complete our work.

53

### **6.1 Summary**

The objectives defined in the introduction section have been attained for the most

part:

The system proposed is simple and close to the SPIDAR in terms of design and

haptic feedback provided.

The control scheme has been thoroughly described and simple is enough to allow

real time force feedback.

The workspace of the systemhas been enlarged to almost all the frame with a gain

of more than half its previous size.

The force feedback is accurate and done without using sensors which reduces the

cost of the interface.

54

### **6.2 Future works**

Future works could incorporate things like a second virtual model in parallel of the

first one in order to simulate both the texture and the movement of the virtual object.

Indeed, the current control law lacks in term of texture rendering when moving the

object. Likewise, the transition between the feedback mode and free movement mode

could be smoothened or suppressed.

But the most important parts that could be improved are the evaluation of feedback

end adapting this work to three dimensions.

**6.2.1** **3D interface**

Adapting our system to three dimensions would require a new model based on what

was presented in this paper in terms of kinematics. Doing so tasks like grasping an

object would be possible.

An example of a possible interface in shown in annex: with the configuration we

propose it would be possible to either do carrying tasks in cooperation or grasping task

with bimanual interaction. Model to improve the cooperation between humans could

also be implemented and tested using this kind of interface and setup.

55

**6.2.2** **User based evaluation**

It was pointed out before that due to a lack of time user based evaluation was not

conducted. This kind of evaluation could not only be interesting to complete the results

presented in this paper, but with a well designed survey, we could apply this type of

evaluation to Human-human interaction.

We summed up the future prospects of our work in figure 6.1 on the next page. The

next objectives are to design a 3D interface to allow bimanual interaction. Once it is

done, we could work on human assit and improve the evaluation of haptic feedback with

a user based evaluation.
