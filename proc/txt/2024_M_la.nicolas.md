# 第 1 章 Introduction

In recent years, researchers have increasingly turned their attention to nature's

ability to solve complex problems, such as optimizing transportation networks. This

section explores various models inspired by natural phenomena and behaviors,

examining how individual interactions drive the self-organization of systems. Building

on these insights, we propose a hybrid multi-agent system designed to create

optimized transportation networks, striking a balance between minimizing the total

network size and reducing travel time.

### **1.1 Research background **

This research focuses on the design of efficient path system that connect critical

points, or hotspots, in a way that optimizes both performance and adaptability to

real-world constraints. These networks are vital across a wide range of applications,

including urban planning, telecommunications, energy distribution, water systems,

traffic prediction, neuroscience, autonomous vehicle navigation, and even game

development.

By leveraging advanced optimization models, we aim to balance competing

objectives such as minimizing construction or operational costs, reducing travel or

transmission time, and ensuring robustness against dynamic demands and

constraints. Such work provides valuable insights and tools for improving the

efficiency and sustainability of complex systems.

### **1.2 Previous research **

To reproduce real-world transport systems and networks, my research employs a

variety of approaches that integrate bio-inspired algorithms, graph theory, and

optimization techniques. These methods aim to achieve a balance between

minimizing total network size, reducing travel times, and ensuring robustness under

real-world constraints. For instance, the application of Steiner trees offers an

effective solution for designing efficient path systems by minimizing the overall

network length while connecting key nodes.

Researchers aimed their attention to other solutions like bio-inspired multi-agent

model and algorithms to solve NP-hard problems, in particular slime mold and

pedestrian movement.

This discussion explores the use of these models and algorithms, evaluating their

performances and the constraints they encounter in real-world applications.

##### **1.2.1 Spanning and Steiner trees **

Steiner trees are a concept in graph theory and network optimization used to

connect a set of points (known as terminal nodes) with the shortest possible total

network length. Unlike direct spanning trees, which connect all points directly

without introducing any new nodes, Steiner trees allow the inclusion of additional

points (called Steiner nodes) to reduce the overall network length.

For example, imagine trying to connect cities with roads to minimize construction

costs. A Steiner tree might introduce intermediate junctions to achieve a more

efficient design, resulting in a shorter total network than if the cities were simply

connected directly.

Intermediate solution for three points arranged in a triangular configuration.

4

Fig. 1.3: (I) Spanning tree solution with direct connections, (II) Steiner tree and (III)
Intermediate solution for four points arranged in a square configuration.

However, Steiner trees are not always ideal for real-world scenarios due to several

practical limitations and the mathematical calculation of Steiner trees can be

complex and approximated [1]. First, Steiner trees focus solely on minimizing

construction or connection costs and do not account for other critical factors such as

travel time, reliability, or adaptability to changing demands. In transportation

networks, for instance, the shortest possible network might result in indirect routes,

leading to increased travel time and reduced practicality for users.

Second, real-world networks often involve additional constraints such as

geographic barriers, political boundaries, or infrastructure requirements, which

Steiner trees do not inherently consider. For example, a road network optimized

purely for minimal length might cross areas where construction is impractical or

prohibited, such as mountains, rivers, or private land.

5

Finally, Steiner trees assume static conditions, whereas real-world systems are

dynamic, with fluctuating demand, traffic patterns, or environmental factors. A

network designed as a Steiner tree may lack the robustness or flexibility needed to

handle disruptions or accommodate growth, making it less suitable for long-term

applications.

To address these challenges, more adaptable solutions are necessary—ones that

balance the cost-efficiency of Steiner trees with the practicality of spanning trees.

These intermediate approaches prioritize not only minimizing network size but also

improve travel time, usability, and resilience to real-world constraints, resulting in

networks better suited for practical applications like transportation,

telecommunications, or urban planning.

Furthermore, a more dynamically adaptable model—capable of self-reconfiguring

until it reaches a stable state within a predefined simulated environment—can

provide a powerful tool for designing optimized transportation networks. Such a

model would allow for the iterative refinement of the network, accounting for factors

like fluctuating demand, geographic constraints, and environmental changes,

ultimately leading to a more robust and efficient system tailored to real-world

conditions.

##### **1.2.2 Slime mold behavior for urban network design**

The slime mold model provides valuable insights into adaptive, self-organizing

systems that mimic efficient resource distribution [2-7]. Based on the natural

behavior of the Physarum Polycephalum, it has inspired the development of a multi
agent algorithm designed to emulate its remarkable ability to create efficient

transport networks. In this system, each agent is modeled with the same morphology

and behavior, mimicking the decentralized and self-organizing characteristics of

slime mold. These agents operate independently, following simple local rules to

explore and connect resource points within an environment.

Fig. 1.4: Physarum Polycephalum's ability to recreate the Tokyo railway system: oats
representing stations and slime mold growth forming an optimized transport network
after 26 hours.

7

When deployed in large numbers, these agents collectively form intricate networks

that optimize path efficiency and resource distribution. Remarkably, this approach

mirrors slime mold's ability to recreate real-world transportation systems, such as the

Tokyo railway network, by finding near-optimal connections between key nodes

while minimizing overall network length. This bio-inspired algorithm demonstrates

how emergent behavior in simple agents can lead to sophisticated, scalable

solutions for complex urban design challenges.

Each agent is composed of four key components, including three sensors—

forward (F), front-right (FR), and front-left (FL)—which detect the concentration of

stimuli in the surrounding environment. These sensors guide the agent's movement

by influencing its position and orientation. Based on the stimuli detected, the agent

either rotates toward the strongest local source of stimuli or continues moving

forward in its current direction.

The behavior of the agents can be fine-tuned by adjusting two critical parameters:

the sensor offset (SO) and the sensor angle (SA).

Sensor Offset (SO) determines the spatial distance between the agent and its

sensors. Increasing the SO value enhances the agent's foraging behavior, leading to

the formation of thicker, more interconnected networks and enabling faster

adaptation to environmental changes. Conversely, a smaller SO reduces foraging

tendencies, promoting grouping behavior and tighter clustering of agents.

Sensor Angle (SA) defines the angular placement of the sensors relative to the

agent's forward direction. Smaller SA values result in agents moving along straighter

paths, which may cause them to bypass food sources or fail to group effectively. In

contrast, larger SA values allow the agent to better detect stimuli from a wider range

of angles, improving its ability to forage and adapt to the environment.

By carefully balancing these parameters, the agents' behaviors can be tailored to

simulate various scenarios, from foraging and exploration to grouping and clustering.

This flexibility makes the model highly adaptable for studying emergent path

systems and their optimization in dynamic environments.

##### **1.2.3 Pedestrian movement to reproduce desire paths**

The pedestrian movement model contributes to understand human dynamics

within networks, capturing the influence of desire paths and real-world behavioral

tendencies [9].

The pedestrian movement behavior implemented in this multi-agent algorithm is

designed to replicate the self-organizing phenomenon of desire paths, facilitating

the creation of efficient, optimized networks. The model is based on simple yet

effective rules inspired by human spatial cognition and movement patterns observed

in urban environments. Each agent in the model represents an individual pedestrian,

equipped with two primary visual parameters – angle of vision and depth of vision.

The interaction between these parameters defines the agent's "viewshed," or the

area within which they perceive and respond to environmental cues and affordances.

Fig. 1.6: Visual parameters of agent in pedestrian movement model

10

Desire paths arise from the repeated passage of individuals at specific locations.

By deploying a large number of agents, each individual interact with their

environment and modify it, ultimately leading to the formation of a global network

pattern. However, a key feature of this model is its dynamic representation of human

movement.

Unlike a simplistic trajectory-following approach, the agents’ orientation is not

fixed solely to their current path. Instead, they direct their view area toward their

destination, regardless of their immediate trajectory. This behavior mimics how

humans—and other living beings—often turn their head or change their focus to

anticipate and navigate their surroundings, even when their current movement is

directed elsewhere. This aspect introduces a higher level of dynamism into the

agents’ behavior, allowing them to adapt their paths more fluidly in response to

both immediate and anticipated environmental factors.

By enabling the agents to orient their attention away from their current trajectory,

the model incorporates a more natural, human-like adaptability. It allows for a more

nuanced and realistic representation of movement, where agents can adjust their

route or make real-time decisions based on new stimuli or emerging traffic patterns

and optimize their journey.

##### **1.2.4 General issues with previous models**

While previous models have made significant strides in replicating natural

behaviors and optimizing network designs, they often face several limitations. These

include a lack of adaptability to dynamic environments, the emergence of

inefficiencies such as incomplete coverage or deviated paths, and challenges in

balancing competing objectives like minimizing network length and ensuring

practical usability. Additionally, while comparisons to real human-designed path

systems have been conducted, there has been little to no evaluation of performance

in terms of total network size and the ability to account for complete travel counts.

This section explores these general issues, highlighting the gaps that need to be

addressed to improve the reliability and applicability of such models in real-world

scenarios.

13

**1.2.4.1** **Slime mold model limitations**

If we analyze the agent behavior algorithm, it becomes apparent that agents tend

to continue facing the same direction, even in the absence of detectable stimulus

concentrations. This behavior can lead to issues such as uncertainty in the direction

of the emerging pattern and the formation of incomplete or faulty regions in the

resulting network or structure.

14

This phenomenon occurs because agents, lacking stimuli to guide their movement,

rely on their previous orientation, causing them to proceed in a straight or slightly

deviated path. While this might simplify local decision-making, it introduces a level

of randomness that can disrupt the coherence of the overall system. For example, in

a simulated transport network, agents may fail to cover critical areas or may diverge

from their intended paths, leading to inefficiencies and gaps in the final design.

Fig. 1.8: Emergence of Steiner tree patterns, deviated paths and region faults in a
multi-agent model simulating slime mold behavior.

Additionally, this behavior highlights the importance of incorporating mechanisms

to correct or adapt agent trajectories in the absence of stimuli. Without such

mechanisms, the self-organization process can suffer from incomplete coverage or

imbalanced resource allocation, which may limit the model’s ability to produce

efficient, optimized patterns.

15

**1.2.4.2** **Desire paths model limitations**

In contrast, the pedestrian movement model offers valuable insights by mimicking

human behavior, but it falls short when it comes to evaluating the stability of patterns,

the performance of the network, or the distribution of road usage frequency. One

significant limitation of this approach is its inability to dynamically adapt over time.

Despite the emergence of a transport network, unused roads persist in the final

output. This static feature hinders the model’s capacity to evolve and adapt to

changing conditions, as it does not account for the diffusion phenomenon—one

where paths dynamically shift based on real-time usage and traffic flow.

The lack of adaptability in the desire paths model is particularly problematic for

long-term urban planning and transportation optimization. Human traffic needs are

constantly fluctuating, influenced by factors such as time of day, weather conditions,

and urban events, which require a system that can evolve in response to these

changes. Without the ability to adjust and redistribute traffic flows in real-time, the

model is restricted in its effectiveness. For example, if certain roads or routes are

underutilized, the system remains static and fails to redirect traffic, which ultimately

reduces the overall efficiency of the network.

A more robust and adaptable model would incorporate a time-dependent variable

and integrate diffusion processes. By simulating how human traffic needs evolve

dynamically, the model could continuously optimize traffic patterns, removing

unused roads from active use and rerouting traffic to maintain a balanced, efficient

system. This approach would ensure that the resulting network remains flexible,

reducing inefficiencies and enhancing performance over time. In applications such

as urban planning, traffic optimization, and resource distribution, the model would

be capable of adjusting to the ongoing changes in traffic demand, making it more

reliable and sustainable for future use.

16

### **2.1 Destination decision-making principle **

Choosing a destination is a complex process influenced by a combination of

personal preferences, external factors, and contextual circumstances. Whether for

leisure, business, or other purposes, individuals and groups rely on various principles

to guide their decisions. These principles can be broadly categorized into the

following:

- Attractiveness

Visit frequency

The attractiveness of a place is one of the most significant factors influencing its

selection as a travel destination. It encompasses elements such as cultural landmarks,

scenic beauty, recreational activities, and the overall ambiance of the location. Highly

attractive destinations naturally draw attention and become popular among travelers.

Places with unique characteristics, such as the Tokyo Skytree’s breathtaking views

or Shinjuku’s vibrant nightlife, hold a strong allure for tourists. The attractiveness of

a location is often shaped by several key factors. Aesthetic appeal, including beautiful

landscapes, architectural marvels, and unique urban designs, plays a significant role in

drawing visitors. Cultural significance, embodied in historical landmarks and cultural

events, adds depth and meaning to a place, making it more compelling. Additionally,

the availability of amenities and activities, such as entertainment, shopping, and

diverse dining options, enhances the overall experience for visitors. Together, these

elements establish high attractiveness, making a place a "must-visit" destination and

encouraging both initial and repeat visits.

However, their frequent visitation and resulting crowd density can inversely affect

their appeal.

19

Visit frequency represents the volume of people visiting a location within a given

period and serves as an indicator of a destination’s popularity. However, while high

visit frequency underscores a place’s appeal, it can also introduce challenges such as

overcrowding, long wait times, and a lack of personal space, all of which may diminish

the overall visitor experience.

Highly crowded destinations often affect the decision-making process for potential

visitors in significant ways. For travelers seeking relaxation or tranquility, the

perception of overcrowding can be a deterrent, prompting them to choose less

frequented locations. Additionally, the reduced quality of experience caused by

overcrowding can manifest in logistical challenges, such as difficulties accessing key

attractions or finding suitable accommodations. Environmental concerns linked to

heavy footfall, including degradation of natural or urban spaces, further compound

these issues, negatively influencing perceptions of the destination.

### **2.3 Target selection algorithm **

A bias-based target selection algorithm has been implemented and generates a

random integer that adheres to certain constraints. Its purpose is to select an index

from the set of hotspots, excluding the hotspot where agent is currently localized,

while considering a weighted probability distribution. The algorithm accounts for the

weights of each index, derived from the human visitation law, and uses a random float

input to determine the selection in a probabilistic manner.

First, the algorithm calculates cumulative weights for all indices, excluding the one

specified. This involves iterating through the list of hotspots and summing the weights

based on conditions that ensure the exclusion of the specified index. Two cumulative

weight variables track ranges—one up to the index before the excluded index, and the

other including the excluded index. These cumulative weights define intervals for

determining whether the randomly generated value falls within the range that would

select the excluded index. If the random value lands in this range, the algorithm adjusts

the selection to avoid the excluded index by returning the next valid index.

If the random value does not fall within the range tied to the excluded index, the

algorithm iterates through all weights again, adding them cumulatively until the

random value matches a particular interval. The index corresponding to this interval is

returned as the selected target. This process ensures that all indices, except the

excluded one, are selectable with probabilities proportional to their weights.

#### **3.1 Agent morphology**

The morphology of an agent refers to its physical or conceptual structure, as well as

its capabilities for perception, interaction, and action within the system. In this specific

model, each agent is composed of three key components: a view area, a distance offset,

and a view area width. These elements define how the agent perceives and interacts

with its environment, guiding its behavior and decision-making.

Fig. 3.1: Agent morphology with parameters.

The view area represents the spatial region within which the agent can observe and

respond to its surroundings. This area determines the scope of environmental data the

agent can process, influencing its ability to identify targets or respond to changes.

The distance offset specifies the relative position of the view area from the agent.

This parameter allows the agent to focus on regions that are not immediately adjacent,

enabling it to anticipate and react to environmental features or interactions further

afield.

27

The view area width defines the angular breadth of the agent's field of view. A

narrower view limits the agent's focus to a specific direction, promoting precise and

targeted behavior. Conversely, a wider view enables the agent to take a broader range

of environmental factors into account.

The inclusion of this focus ability parameter enriches the model by adding a layer of

flexibility and adaptability to agent behavior. It allows for the exploration of scenarios

where agents must dynamically balance focus and awareness, depending on their

objectives and environmental demands. This adaptability leads to more realistic

simulations, as real-world entities often need to shift between narrow focus (e.g.,

pursuing a target) and broader awareness (e.g., scanning for threats or opportunities).

In a self-organizing system, the ability to modulate the view area width can enhance

emergent behaviors. For instance, agents with a narrow focus might excel at goal
directed tasks, while those with a wider focus might contribute to group-level

coordination and environmental exploration. This diversity in agent behavior, enabled

by the focus ability, drives richer and more robust emergent patterns in the overall

system.

Together, these morphological elements equip the agent to operate autonomously

and dynamically within the system. By perceiving its immediate environment through

its view area, adjusting its focus with the distance offset, and modulating its attention

using the view area width, the agent forms the basis for complex, coordinated

behaviors that emerge at the system level.

#### **3.3 From local information to self-organization**

The behavior of individual agents, governed by simple local interactions, can

collectively lead to the emergence of global self-organization within a system. Each

agent operates autonomously, sensing its immediate surroundings, responding to

environmental stimuli such as pheromones, and making decisions based on localized

information. These decentralized interactions are influenced by the deployment of a

large number of agents, which amplifies the effect of individual actions on the overall

system.

As agents deposit pheromones along their paths, they create trails that encode their

movements and decisions. Other agents sense and respond to these pheromone trails,

reinforcing well-traveled paths while exploring less-visited regions. This positive

feedback loop strengthens effective routes, allowing the system to converge on paths

that provide optimal connectivity between important locations. At the same time,

pheromone dissipation prevents the system from becoming overly rigid, enabling it

to adapt dynamically to changing conditions or obstacles.

This self-organizing behavior is particularly powerful for creating optimized path

systems. By deploying a large number of agents, the system can efficiently explore vast

and complex environments, identifying multiple potential routes. Over time, the

interaction between agents and their environment naturally selects the shortest, most

efficient, or most resource-effective paths. The emergent paths are not pre-defined

but arise from the collective actions of agents, making the system highly robust and

adaptable.

#### **4.4.1   Path efficiency **

The efficiency of road networks is a critical aspect of evaluating transportation

systems. Efficiency can be assessed for individual routes and the overall path system

by examining the relationship between the direct route distance and the travel

distance along constructed roads. While a single route may appear less efficient

compared to its direct counterpart, the shared use of roads within a larger network

often minimizes the total length of the road system, providing a more resource
efficient solution overall.

the travel distance equals the direct route distance. Lower values indicate greater

deviations from the direct path, often resulting from the constraints of shared road

usage, terrain, or urban planning considerations.

In real-world applications, road networks rarely consist of perfectly direct routes.

Instead, paths are optimized for shared usage and resource allocation, balancing

efficiency with practicality. For example, constructing perfectly direct roads between

every pair of destinations would lead to an unsustainable increase in total road length

and construction costs. By sharing segments of roads across multiple routes, the

overall length of the road system is reduced, even if individual routes deviate from the

most direct paths.

In this research, this balance between individual route efficiency and system-wide

optimization is a fundamental focus. The modeling framework I developed

demonstrates how the dynamic interactions of agents and pheromone-based

mechanisms can create self-organized networks that prioritize shared usage while

maintaining acceptable levels of individual route efficiency. The resulting networks

strike a balance between minimizing the total system length and ensuring that travel

times remain competitive.

This approach provides insights into how transportation systems can be designed

to maximize resource efficiency while maintaining high functionality. By

understanding and quantifying route efficiency and its relationship to the larger

system, we can better inform the development of road networks and other shared

infrastructure systems.

### **5.2 Complete travels **

The performance of complete travels varies significantly across different path

configurations, highlighting the trade-offs inherent in the system. When comparing

Steiner tree patterns to direct route configurations, we observe notable differences

in the number of complete travels achieved over the simulation period.

In a Steiner tree configuration, individual paths tend to be longer compared to

direct routes. This is because Steiner trees prioritize the optimization of the overall

system by creating shared paths that connect multiple hotspots. While this approach

reduces the total length of the path system and minimizes redundancy, it inherently

increases the travel distance for individual agents. Given that the velocity of agents

and the length of each generation time remain constant throughout the simulation,

longer paths result in agents taking more time to complete a single travel.

Consequently, the total number of complete travels achieved in the same time

period is reduced in Steiner tree configurations.

In contrast, direct route configurations focus on the shortest path between each

agent's origin and destination. These simpler, more direct paths allow agents to

complete their travels more quickly, leading to a higher total number of complete

travels within the same simulation period. However, this increase in individual

efficiency comes at the expense of overall system optimization, as the path system

becomes less compact and more redundant.

### **5.3 Evaluation limitations **

The previous series of simulations provided valuable insights into the various path

system patterns that can emerge under specific configurations. These results

allowed us to evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of different agent

morphologies in generating efficient and functional solutions. However, it is

important to note that the presented graphs and analyses are specifically based on

an equilateral triangular configuration of hotspots.

The spatial distribution and number of hotspots play a crucial role in shaping the

performance of the path system. A uniform and symmetrical arrangement, such as

the equilateral triangle configuration, naturally facilitates the formation of optimized

patterns like Steiner trees, as agents can easily identify and share common paths. In

contrast, an irregular or asymmetrical distribution of hotspots introduces additional

complexity, potentially leading to less predictable patterns and varying degrees of

efficiency.

These variations in hotspot distribution and density highlight the adaptability and

limitations of different agent configurations. As noticed from previous simulation

example, a deadlock in the resulting pattern can occur. Certain agent setups, such as

those with a high angle and depth of view, may perform well in symmetrical

configurations by efficiently consolidating paths, but their effectiveness may

diminish in irregular layouts. Conversely, configurations with limited visibility may

excel in environments with clearly defined and localized hotspots but struggle in

larger or more dispersed networks.

Future studies should explore the impact of different hotspot arrangements on

agent behavior and path system performance.

### **6.2 Future work and potential improvements **

Building on the findings of this study, several future research can be explored to

enhance the model and its application. One potential direction is to integrate real
world data, such as the transportation patterns in Tokyo, to compare to our model

accuracy and applicability. By analyzing origin-destination data from train users, it is

possible to extract probability distributions for selecting destinations based on the

starting point. These probabilities could then be applied to hotspots within the

simulation, representing train stations, providing a more realistic simulation of

commuter behavior. Comparing the resulting path system generated by the model

with the actual railway system would offer valuable insights into the accuracy and

efficiency of the simulated network, as well as potential areas for optimization.

However, for better accuracy, an auto-balancing traffic model has to be

implemented.

Additionally, further investigation could be conducted by experimenting with

different weight functions for the pixels, which would affect how agents evaluate

paths and make decisions. This could involve testing the impact of varying the weight

functions on the performance of the model, providing a deeper understanding of how

different factors influence the overall behavior and efficiency of the path system.

In terms of improving the model's realism, an enhancement could be made to the

agent behavior algorithm. Currently, the model assumes a straight-line approach,

where agents follow the shortest direct path between origin and destination. An

interesting improvement would be to integrate an A* pathfinding algorithm, which

would enable agents to take into account not only the distance but also other factors

such as obstacles or varying terrain. This would make the agents' behavior more

nuanced and closer to real-world pathfinding, where multiple factors influence the

choice of route.

64

Furthermore, incorporating a terrain map could allow agents to adjust their routes

based on accessibility and environmental constraints. For example, agents would

need to avoid areas that are inaccessible or too costly to traverse, such as mountains

or bodies of water. While this improvement would introduce more complexity to the

model, it would also create a more sophisticated simulation that better represents

real-world challenges in route selection and optimization. However, this would

require handling more advanced scenarios where agents must make decisions based

on their surroundings and the availability of paths, which would add an extra layer of

complexity to the simulation.

Another potential enhancement involves utilizing the agent morphology, which

allows changes to the focus distance and angle. By dynamically adjusting these

parameters according to the agents' "changing" environment as they move, the

model can better reproduce human adaptation behavior. This adaptation mechanism

could enable agents to respond more effectively to environmental variations and

uncertainties, creating a simulation that more accurately mirrors real-world human

decision-making processes.
